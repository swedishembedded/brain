# Volume XI: Forecasting, Decisions and Uncertainty

Every decision that matters is made before its consequences are known. A grid operator commits generators for tomorrow from a guess at tomorrow's demand; a shop orders stock for a month it has not seen; a clinic books beds for patients who have not yet arrived; a trader sizes a position against a price that has not moved. In each case the useful object is not a number but a statement about the future that is honest about what it does not know, and a way of telling, long after the event, whether the statement deserved trust. A forecaster that is right on average and confidently wrong on the days that cost money is worse than none, and one whose claims are never checked against what happened is not a forecaster at all.

That puts three questions in one volume. The first is what is special about data that arrives in order: values that depend on their neighbours, patterns that repeat on a calendar, statistics that drift, and a split between what is known and what is to come that every honest evaluation has to respect. The second is how a prediction should carry its own uncertainty, as a set of quantiles or a full distribution, and how it is scored when the truth finally arrives. The third is what to do with a prediction: a threshold on a probability, a price on a risk, a decision whose cost is asymmetric. Between them lies a modern development, models pretrained on enormous collections of series that forecast ones they have never seen, and the question of how to tell skill from luck when such a model is compared with a plain baseline.

The volume follows that line. It starts from a small series that can be reproduced by hand, builds the baselines and the autoregressive model that every later method is measured against, and fixes the rules for cutting a series into windows without letting the future in. It then turns the forecast into a distribution, with the quantile loss and the scores that reward calibrated spread. It looks inside the pretrained forecasters, which cut a series into patches or turn it into discrete tokens before a transformer reads it. It sets out how a forecaster is evaluated across many origins and how a difference between two of them is tested. It then turns to a different kind of target, the time until something happens when some of the cases have not yet happened, and to the scoring rules and thresholds with which probabilities become decisions.

## Why it comes here

The volume leans on the ones before it, and most of all on the first two. Volume I supplied the probability, the covariance, the statistics of samples and their standard errors, the Fourier transform that finds a period, and the generator that makes every example reproducible. Volume II supplied least squares, the error metrics, calibration, the split of data into training and test sets and the leakage that spoils it, and the discipline of baselines, seeds and honest claims; a forecast evaluated carelessly is one of the cleanest cases of the leak that volume described. Volume V supplied the transformer block and Volume VII the patch embedding, which together are the body of a pretrained forecaster, and Volume VIII supplied the discrete codebook that one of them uses to turn a series into tokens. Volumes IX and X dealt with signals sampled in time, sound and video, and the question of what a sampling rate does; here the signals are the plain numbers of business and science, and the same care about the time axis carries over.

The order follows the dependencies. Nothing about forecasting is safe until the structure of a series and the rule for splitting it are fixed, so the volume begins there. A forecast that is more than a point needs a way to be scored, which comes next, and the pretrained models need that scoring before their claims can be read. Evaluation across many origins and the testing of a difference between two forecasters come after the models, because it is the models that make the question urgent. Survival analysis shares the statistics but changes the target, and the scoring rules of the last chapter generalise what the volume has used throughout. The volume ends with a project that builds a forecaster whose uncertainty is calibrated and whose evaluation is honest, which is the thing the preceding chapters take apart.

## What you can do after it

You can say what separates a time series from a table of rows, read its memory and its season out of an autocorrelation and a spectrum, decide what to difference, and build the naive, seasonal and autoregressive forecasts that a model has to beat. You can cut a series into windows and into training and test sets so that no bar a model is judged on was ever used in fitting it, and scale a series by what came before it. You can state a forecast as quantiles, train it with the pinball loss, score it with the continuous ranked probability score, and check whether its intervals contain the truth as often as they claim. You can describe how a pretrained forecaster tokenises and conditions a series, evaluate it by walking forward through time, and test a difference in skill without being fooled by luck or by the number of models tried. You can estimate a survival curve when some outcomes are unobserved, model a hazard, and separate competing causes. And you can score probabilities with a rule that rewards honesty and set the threshold at which a probability becomes an action.
