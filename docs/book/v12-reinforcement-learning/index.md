# Volume XII: Reinforcement Learning and Continuous Improvement

A spelling checker is graded on a fixed list of words, and so is almost every model of the volumes before this one: the examples exist before the model does, and nothing the model says changes them. Three situations break that arrangement. A shopkeeper who orders too little today has an empty shelf tomorrow, and an empty shelf is a different problem from a full one. A recommender that shows one film changes what the viewer will ask for next. A language model that writes an answer is graded on the answer it wrote, by a checker that may only run after the last word, and the answers it will be trained on tomorrow are the ones it writes today. In each case the learner's own choices make the data it will see, and the only feedback is a number that can arrive long after the choice that earned it.

That puts three questions in one volume. The first is how to describe such a problem exactly: what a state is, what an action does to it, what a reward is paid for, and what it means to prefer a stream of rewards to another. Once the description is exact the best behaviour can be computed, at least for problems small enough to write down, and the computation fixes the vocabulary of everything after it. The second is how to improve behaviour when the description is not available, either by estimating what states are worth from experience or by nudging the policy itself so that actions followed by good outcomes become more likely; and how those ideas, applied to a pretrained language model with a checker or a pool of human preferences standing in for the world, turn a text predictor into an assistant. The third is what to do around the learner: when to search instead of learn, how a small trained model makes fast and auditable decisions inside an application, and how a model that keeps learning from new experience is kept from forgetting and from being replaced by something worse.

The volume follows that line. It starts with a shop whose every number can be printed, defines the decision process, the return, the value of a state and the equations that tie the values together, and solves the shop exactly. It then drops the table of probabilities and learns values from experience, with the exploration that the two-button game of Volume II only hinted at. It turns to policy gradients, the baseline and advantage that make them usable, and the clipped objective of PPO. It applies those tools to language models, from supervised fine-tuning through preference data, reward models and direct preference optimisation to group-relative methods with rewards that a program can verify. It sets search beside learning, with archives that keep many different good solutions and goals that are relabelled after the fact. It builds the small decision models that run inside an application. And it ends with continual learning and the gate that decides when a new version may replace the old one, before a project that trains an agent from verified feedback.

## Why it comes here

The volume leans on the ones before it, and on the first three most of all. Volume I supplied probability, expectation and conditional probability, which are the whole language in which a decision process is defined, and the divergences and log-probabilities that the policy methods of the middle chapters need. Volume II supplied the first look at reinforcement learning, the two-button game in which an agent's choices decide the data it gets, and with it the habits of honest evaluation: a validation set, a paired comparison, a claim measured against a baseline. Volume III supplied stochastic gradient descent and the networks that a value or a policy is eventually made of. Volumes V and VI supplied the causal language model, the chat template and the masking of a loss, which are the body of the language-model chapters, and the sentence embeddings with which a small decision model reads its input. Volume XI is the neighbour on the other side: forecasting asked what a model can say about a future it does not influence, and this volume asks what to do when it does.

The order follows the dependencies. Nothing about learning from reward is precise until the problem is, so the volume begins with the decision process and its equations. Values learned from experience need those equations, because the update they make is a sample of one; policy gradients need the policy and the return, and the language-model methods need the clipped objective of the policy-gradient chapter. Search takes the decision process back as its object, now with planning in place of learning, and the decision models, the continual learner and the promotion gate each need the pieces above them. The volume ends with a project that builds an agent whose improvement is measured, which is the point of everything before it.

## What you can do after it

You can write a small decision problem as states, actions, transition probabilities and rewards, compute the return of a trajectory under any discount, solve for the value of a policy by a linear system or by sweeps, and find the best policy by value iteration or policy iteration, with the number of sweeps predicted before the run. You can learn values from sampled experience without a model of the world, and say what exploration costs. You can write the policy-gradient estimator, subtract a baseline without changing its mean, and explain what a clipped objective protects. You can turn a pretrained language model into one that follows instructions, from the loss on demonstrations to a reward model, a preference loss and a verifiable reward, and you can say what the penalty against the starting model is for. You can tell when search beats a learned policy and when the two help each other, build a small decision model that gives a calibrated answer quickly, and decide, with a stated test, whether a retrained model may replace the one in use.
