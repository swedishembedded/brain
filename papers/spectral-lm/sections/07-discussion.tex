\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the mixer does well.} With the parameter count of a SwiGLU
whose hidden width equals the channel count, the spectral mixer predicts
held-out text at least as well and, on every seed we ran, better. Its
parameters are per-mode gains, but its computation is that of a much wider
layer: each channel synthesizes a $D$-sample signal and multiplies two of them,
which couples every pair of retained modes through spectral convolution. The
mixer therefore trades parameters for arithmetic and activation memory. Where
parameters are the binding constraint (storage, a parameter budget for
adapters, memory traffic of weights during decoding), that trade can be the
right one.

\paragraph{What it does not do.} The trade is unfavourable when arithmetic is
the constraint. At about the same analytic arithmetic per token a dense
SwiGLU spends it on four and a half times as many parameters and is far
better, and the implementation makes the gap larger: the spectral model trains
at half the control's speed and holds several times its memory, because its
signal-domain activations are $C\cdot D$ wide per token. The fast transform
recovers only a small part of its arithmetic advantage over the dense basis
product with our kernels. Nothing measured here supports the hope that a
Fourier representation of the hidden state makes a language model cheaper to
compute.

\paragraph{Why a pretrained model cannot simply be truncated.} The
residual-stream study (\cref{sec:res-compress}) closes the other route: a
pretrained model's coordinates carry no frequency order, so its lowest
Fourier modes are about as uninformative as random directions, as
\cref{prop:absorption} predicts. A spectral representation, if it is useful
at all, has to be learned, as SpectralLM learns it, not imposed on a trained
model.

\paragraph{Alternative explanations.} The learning rate was chosen with one
seed per rate over 300 steps, and SpectralLM's losses were not monotone in the
rate, so its selected rate may not be its best; a better rate could only
widen its advantage over the parameter-matched control, but it could also
narrow its gap to the arithmetic-matched one. The channel pilots suggest that
quality saturates near $C=24$ at the selected rate, but one seed per pilot
cannot separate saturation from seed variation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The comparisons are at small scale: decoders of one to twenty-four million
non-embedding parameters, one corpus of simple synthetic English, one token
budget, one context length, and single-precision training on Tesla P40
cards. They say nothing about how the gaps change with scale, with a broader
corpus, or with a longer context. Three seeds per primary model give wide
intervals; non-inferiority is established, superiority is not. The pretrained
reference was continued for an eighth of the trained models' token budget at a
conventional rate that was not tuned. The transform-cost measurements are of
our kernels on one card and do not bound what a better FFT implementation
could reach. The mixer is one member of a larger family; channel-mixing
spectral weights, sparse cross-frequency terms and dense escape channels were
not evaluated, and neither was spectral structure along the sequence axis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A Fourier basis changes what a language model computes only through operators
defined relative to it. With such an operator, a feed-forward sublayer of
per-mode filters around a signal-domain gated product, a decoder of the same
width and parameter count as a Qwen3 control predicts held-out text at least
as well, but a dense feed-forward network with the same arithmetic is clearly
better, faster and smaller in memory. The spectral hidden state is a way to
spend fewer parameters, not less compute. The most consequential open
question is whether the same structure on the sequence axis, where attention's
pairwise cost lives, can move the quality-per-compute frontier that the
feature axis did not. \StatusConclusion
